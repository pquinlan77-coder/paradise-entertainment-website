VETERANS' MIGRAINE DOCUMENTATION GUIDE

How to Document Migraines for VA Disability Claims

A practical guide to recording your migraine history using the actual federal rating criteria and the Department of Veterans Affairs' Headaches Disability Benefits Questionnaire (DBQ).

Source review: November 19, 2026. This guide is educational and is not legal or medical advice. It is not affiliated with or endorsed by the VA.

Important distinction: 38 U.S.C. § 1155 authorizes VA's disability rating schedule. The migraine-specific evaluation criteria are in 38 CFR § 4.124a, Diagnostic Code 8100. An episode diary can help describe symptoms and impairment, but it does not by itself establish a diagnosis, service connection, or a disability rating.

1. What does federal law say about VA migraine ratings?

38 U.S.C. § 1155 authorizes disability ratings based, as far as practicable, on the average impairment of earning capacity. The specific migraine schedule appears in 38 CFR § 4.124a, Diagnostic Code 8100.

RatingCriteria in Diagnostic Code 8100
50%Very frequent, completely prostrating and prolonged attacks productive of severe economic inadaptability.
30%Characteristic prostrating attacks averaging once a month over the last several months.
10%Characteristic prostrating attacks averaging once every two months over the last several months.
0%Less frequent attacks.

These are the regulatory evaluation levels, not an automatic scoring formula. Frequency alone does not establish the 50% criteria; attack severity, duration, and economic impact also matter.

2. What does “prostrating” mean?

Section IV of the VA's Headaches DBQ describes a prostrating attack as involving extreme exhaustion, powerlessness, debilitation or incapacitation, with a substantial inability to perform ordinary activities. The form asks separately about characteristic prostrating attacks and completely prostrating, prolonged attacks.

In your own records, describe what you actually could and could not do: for example, whether you needed to stop an activity, lie down in a dark room, or miss work. Do not label an attack prostrating solely because it was painful, and do not describe incapacity you did not experience.

3. What should a migraine diary record?

The official DBQ asks a healthcare provider about diagnosis, medical history, headache pain, associated symptoms, typical duration, prostrating attacks, and functional impact. To help you discuss these topics accurately with a healthcare professional, record:

  1. Date and timing: When the episode began, when it ended, and its duration. If the end time is estimated or corrected later, say so.
  2. Pain and associated symptoms: Severity; location; throbbing or constant pain; nausea; vomiting; sensitivity to light or sound; visual or sensory changes, if present.
  3. Activity limitations: What you had to stop doing, whether you required rest, and whether you could carry out ordinary tasks.
  4. Impact on work: Missed hours, inability to complete duties, interruptions, or accommodations actually needed. Specific examples are more useful than broad labels.
  5. Treatment and context: Medication taken, treatment sought, and possible triggers, where known. Do not confuse suspected triggers with medically established causes.

The DBQ's typical-head-pain duration question distinguishes less than one day, one to two days, and more than two days. Accurate start and end times can help describe an individual episode before your clinician summarizes the overall pattern.

4. Why do multiple months of records matter?

Diagnostic Code 8100 evaluates characteristic prostrating attacks over the last several months. The DBQ similarly asks for average frequency across that period, including less frequent attacks, once in two months, once monthly, or greater than once monthly.

Record episodes as they happen and review your history across multiple months. Distinguish an ordinary headache or migraine episode from one that substantially prevented normal activities. Do not assume every logged migraine is a prostrating attack.

5. How should work and economic impact be documented?

The 50% regulatory criterion expressly refers to severe economic inadaptability. Section VII of the VA Headaches DBQ asks whether a headache condition affects the veteran's ability to work and requests examples when it does.

Write down actual work consequences, such as missed shifts, leaving early, reduced ability to perform specific duties, or adjustments made during an episode. Record dates and amounts where known. The guide does not presume that unemployment is required or that any single work interruption establishes a particular rating.

6. Does a migraine diary prove service connection?

No. Symptom and functional-impact documentation can help describe the current condition and its severity. Whether migraines are service-connected, including as secondary to another condition, is a separate determination that may require medical and other evidence. A diary does not establish medical causation or replace a clinician's findings.

VA's Headaches DBQ is intended to be completed by a healthcare provider. Use your personal records to support accurate conversations; do not present an app export as a completed DBQ.

Frequently asked questions

Does every migraine count as a prostrating attack?

No. The VA DBQ gives a specific description of prostrating attacks. Document the actual limitations associated with each episode and leave medical or rating determinations to the appropriate professionals.

Is a 50% VA migraine rating based on attacks happening more than once monthly?

No. The 50% criteria additionally address completely prostrating, prolonged attacks and severe economic inadaptability. See Diagnostic Code 8100 for the complete wording.

Can digital migraine records help prepare for an examination?

They may help you recall dates, symptoms, and concrete examples of functional impact. Your provider and VA determine what additional records or examinations are necessary.
